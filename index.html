Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection (20 Points)

Walk through the ray generation and primitive intersection parts of the rendering pipeline.

For every pixel, raytrace_pixel takes ns_aa samples. Each sample is a random point inside the pixel, found by adding an offset from the uniform grid sampler to the pixel's bottom left corner. We divide by the width and height of the sample buffer to get normalized image coordinates in [0,1], and pass them to Camera::generate_ray.

In camera space the sensor is the plane z = -1, with its bottom left corner at (-tan(hFov/2), -tan(vFov/2), -1) and its top right corner at (tan(hFov/2), tan(vFov/2), -1). We map (x, y) to the point (2x - 1) tan(hFov/2), (2y - 1) tan(vFov/2), -1 on that plane, which gives the ray direction from the camera at the origin. We then rotate the direction into world space with c2w and normalize it. The ray starts at the camera position, and we set its min_t and max_t to the near and far clip distances.

est_radiance_global_illumination passes the ray to the scene's intersect. Triangles and spheres both return true only if the hit t lies between min_t and max_t. On a hit they set max_t to that t, so any later primitive can only report a closer hit, and they fill in the Intersection with t, the surface normal, a pointer to the primitive and its BSDF. After all primitives have been tested, the Intersection holds the nearest hit. For a sphere we solve the quadratic |o + td - c|2 = r2 and use the smaller root if it is in range, otherwise the larger one (the ray starts inside the sphere). The normal is the normalized vector from the center to the hit point. At this stage a pixel's color is the surface normal at the hit, black on a miss, averaged over its samples.


Explain the triangle intersection algorithm you implemented in your own words.

We use the Moller-Trumbore algorithm. A point on the triangle can be written as p1 + b1 (p2 - p1) + b2 (p3 - p1), where b1 and b2 are barycentric coordinates. Setting that equal to the ray point o + t d gives three equations in the three unknowns t, b1 and b2. With e1 = p2 - p1, e2 = p3 - p1, s = o - p1, s1 = d x e2 and s2 = s x e1, Cramer's rule gives det = s1 · e1, t = (s2 · e2) / det, b1 = (s1 · s) / det and b2 = (s2 · d) / det. If det is nearly zero the ray is parallel to the triangle, so we report a miss. Otherwise we compute b0 = 1 - b1 - b2 and count the hit only if b0, b1 and b2 are all at least 0 (the point is inside the triangle) and t is between min_t and max_t. On a hit we update max_t, and the normal is b0 n1 + b1 n2 + b2 n3, normalized, which gives smooth shading across the triangle. We like this method because it solves for the plane hit and the barycentric coordinates in one step, without a separate plane intersection and inside test.

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy (20 Points)

Walk through your BVH construction algorithm. Explain the heuristic you chose for picking the splitting point.

We build the tree recursively over a range of the primitive list. For each range we compute the bounding box of all its primitives and the bounding box of their centroids. If the range has at most max_leaf_size primitives (4 by default) we make a leaf that stores the start and end iterators of the range. Otherwise we make an interior node, split the range in two, and build the left and right children from the two halves.

To split, we pick the axis along which the centroid box is longest, and use std::nth_element to put the half of the primitives with the smaller centroid coordinate on that axis in the left child and the rest in the right child. This is a median split. We chose it over splitting at the midpoint or the average of the centroids because those can put every primitive on one side, for example when many centroids coincide. The recursion would then never shrink and the program would crash. A median split always gives two non-empty halves, so the recursion always ends, and it also keeps the tree balanced with a depth of about log2 n. We use the centroid box instead of the primitive box to choose the axis so that one large primitive does not decide it. The downside is that a median split ignores empty space and primitive sizes, so a surface area heuristic could build a tighter tree.

Compare rendering times on a few scenes with moderately complex geometries with and without BVH acceleration. Present your results in a one-paragraph analysis.

We rendered each scene at 800x600 with 8 threads (-t 8 -r 800 600). For the runs without a BVH we built the BVH with one leaf holding every primitive, which is the same as testing every primitive for every ray. Without the BVH, banana.dae (2,458 primitives) took 0.26 s, cow.dae (5,856) took 1.17 s, maxplanck.dae (50,801) took 13.9 s and CBlucy.dae (133,796) took 24.0 s. With the BVH they took about 0.034 s, 0.037 s, 0.041 s and 0.037 s (the average of three runs each). The average number of primitive tests per ray shows why. Without the BVH it was 597, 2,537, 24,556 and 44,293, and it grows with the number of triangles, so render time grows roughly linearly with scene size (cow to maxplanck is about 8.7 times more triangles and about 12 times the time). With the BVH it was 1.8, 3.8, 4.7 and 2.6, so a ray only reaches a few leaves, and the time barely changes as the scene gets bigger. The speedup goes from about 8 times on banana to about 650 times on CBlucy. The BVH times are mostly fixed costs such as starting the threads and counting rays, so the speedups are lower bounds, and building the BVH for CBlucy took only 0.05 s.


Part 3: Direct Illumination (20 Points)

Walk through both implementations of the direct lighting function.

Both estimators start from the same setup. We build a matrix from the hit normal so that the normal is (0, 0, 1) in a local frame, use it to move directions between the local and world frames, and take hit_p = r.o + t d and w_out in the local frame. est_radiance_global_illumination returns the zero-bounce radiance plus the one-bounce radiance. Zero-bounce radiance is just the emission of the BSDF at the hit, which is what makes the light visible. one_bounce_radiance calls the hemisphere or the importance estimator depending on the -H flag. The diffuse BSDF is f = reflectance / pi. Both estimators approximate the reflection equation, the integral over the hemisphere of f(w_out, w_in) Li cos(θ) dw_in.

For uniform hemisphere sampling we take ns_area_light samples per light. For each one we draw a local direction w_in uniformly from the hemisphere, turn it into a world direction, and cast a ray from hit_p with min_t set to EPS_F so it does not hit the surface it starts on. If the ray hits something, Li is the emission of that surface, which is zero unless we hit a light. Each sample adds f(w_out, w_in) Li cos(θ) / pdf, where cos(θ) is the z component of w_in and the pdf is 1 / (2 pi). We divide by the number of samples at the end.

For importance sampling we loop over the lights instead of the hemisphere. A point or directional light gets one sample, and any other light gets ns_area_light samples. sample_L gives us the radiance, a world space direction to the light, the distance to it and the pdf. If the direction points below the surface in the local frame (z <= 0), the light is behind the surface and we skip it. Otherwise we cast a shadow ray with min_t = EPS_F and max_t = distToLight - EPS_F, so that it can only be blocked by something between the point and the light. If it is not blocked, we add f(w_out, w_in) Li cos(θ) / pdf. We average the samples for each light and then add up the lights.

Compare the results between uniform hemisphere sampling and lighting sampling in a one-paragraph analysis.

At the same number of samples, light sampling is much cleaner. In the 64 sample per pixel, 32 light sample images, the hemisphere renders are still grainy on the walls, the floor and the bunny, while the light sampled ones are smooth. The reason is that hemisphere sampling picks directions blindly, and the light covers a small part of the hemisphere, so most rays miss it and contribute nothing. The few that hit give a large value, which makes the image noisy. Light sampling sends every ray toward the light, so the only samples that contribute nothing are the ones in shadow. Hemisphere sampling also cannot handle point or directional lights at all, because a random direction has zero chance of hitting them. For example, the dragon scene uses a directional light and ambient light, and it came out black with -H. The light sampled images also have no glow around the light panel, which the hemisphere images do, since the area light only emits from one side when we sample it directly. Light sampling is also not slower: on CBspheres_lambertian.dae the hemisphere render took 32.7 s and the light sampled one took 21.9 s, so we get a cleaner image in less time.


Part 4: Global Illumination (20 Points)

Walk through your implementation of the indirect lighting function.

We start each camera ray with depth = max_ray_depth. At the first hit, est_radiance_global_illumination adds the surface's emission when we are accumulating bounces, then calls at_least_one_bounce_radiance to estimate reflected light. A depth of 0 returns only emission, and a depth of 1 gives the direct illumination from Part 3. For larger depths we can follow light that has bounced off other surfaces before reaching this point.

At each hit we build the same local frame as in Part 3, with the normal along the z axis, and put the outgoing direction into that frame. DiffuseBSDF::sample_f uses the cosine-weighted hemisphere sampler to choose an incoming direction w_in, writes its pdf, and returns reflectance / pi. We rotate that direction into world space, normalize it, and cast a new ray from the hit point with min_t = EPS_F and one less depth. If the ray hits another surface, we recursively estimate the reflected light there. Its contribution at the current hit is f * L_in * w_in.z / (pdf * continuation_probability). This accounts for the material, the cosine term and the probability of choosing the path. We do not add the next surface's emission separately, since light-source hits are already covered by our direct lighting estimator.

With isAccumBounces = true, each call adds its direct lighting estimate to the light from the recursive call, so we collect every bounce up to the depth limit. With it set to false, only the call with depth 1 adds direct lighting. The other calls just pass its contribution back through the materials, which isolates the requested mth bounce. We also leave out the camera-visible emission in this mode unless m = 0. For Russian roulette we always trace the first indirect bounce when the maximum depth is greater than 1, then continue each later recursive step with probability 0.65. Surviving contributions are divided by 0.65 to account for the paths that terminate. The depth limit still applies, but we avoid following every path all the way to that limit.

Part 5: Adaptive Sampling (20 Points)

Explain adaptive sampling. Walk through your implementation of the adaptive sampling.

Until now, every pixel took the same number of samples, even if its value had already settled down. Adaptive sampling lets each pixel stop when its estimate is stable enough. A pixel looking at the light or the black background tends to get the same value each time, so it needs few samples. A pixel in a soft shadow or on a surface lit mostly by indirect light can get very different values from different paths, so it needs more. We spend more of the sampling work on these difficult areas instead of giving every pixel the maximum number of samples.

We implement this in raytrace_pixel. Each sample still generates a random camera ray, traces it through the scene and adds its RGB radiance to the pixel's sum. We also call illum() on the radiance to get one illuminance value x, and keep two running sums: s1 += x and s2 += x * x. After n samples the mean is mu = s1 / n, and the sample variance is variance = (s2 - s1 * s1 / n) / (n - 1). This lets us estimate the variance without storing every sample. We clamp it to at least zero in case floating point rounding makes it slightly negative, and only check it once we have at least two samples.

Every samplesPerBatch samples, we calculate I = 1.96 * sqrt(variance / n), the approximate 95% confidence interval's half-width for the mean. We stop if I <= maxTolerance * mu. In our renders the batch size is 64 and the tolerance is 0.05, so the interval must be no more than 5% of the current mean. Checking once per batch avoids doing the square root and convergence test for every ray. If the pixel does not pass the test, we keep sampling until it reaches ns_aa, which is now a maximum rather than a fixed count.

At the end we divide the RGB sum by the actual number of samples taken, and write that same count into sampleCountBuffer. Dividing by the maximum instead would make pixels that stop early too dark. The saved sampling-rate image uses the actual count divided by the maximum: blue means fewer samples, green is around half the budget, and red means the full budget. A constant black pixel can stop at the first batch because both its mean and variance are zero. Noisy dark pixels can take much longer, since their allowed error is small relative to their mean.
